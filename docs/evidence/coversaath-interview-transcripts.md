## Researcher notes — Round 2 (after Transcripts 1–3)

Written 2026-09-03, after transcription. Not part of the transcripts.

**1. The failure is never the absence of insurance. It's the absence of *your own numbers*.**
Arnab's line at [00:09:44] is the whole product thesis and I didn't have it before this round: "On the internet you get the definition. You don't get your number." He had ten lakh of cover and lost eighty thousand to a twelve-hundred-rupee decision he made while emotional, with no information, in a corridor. Content doesn't fix that. A general explainer doesn't fix that. Only *his* extracted, per-person, per-policy numbers fix that. That is mode one, and it has to be per member, not a total. If I had shown Arnab "₹15 lakh total cover" I would have made his October worse, not better.

**2. The four-day window before a planned or semi-planned procedure is the real product moment.**
Not the 2 a.m. emergency I originally designed for. Both Arnab and Vikas independently described the same four-day shape: two days lost to documents, two days to pre-auth. Everything that determined the financial outcome — room choice, which policy went first, how much cash to bring — was decided inside those four days, by people who had no information. That's mode two, and Vikas at [00:02:57] confirmed it changes his job too. Notably he also drew the line I need to respect: it's a persuasion and preparation tool, not a filing document. He files off the policy copy. I should never imply otherwise.

**3. Multi-policy interaction is invisible to users, and it costs them real money.**
Arnab never once considered his employer policy for his mother [00:07:43]. He didn't know a balance amount can go to a second policy [00:11:55]. Vikas says two policy numbers get presented "two or four times a year" and those are the claims that go smoothly [00:04:44]. This is the USP and it is now evidenced, not asserted: nobody — not the insurer, not the aggregator, not the employer platform, not the hospital desk — is structurally able to look at all of a household's sources at once and sequence them. The dashboard is table stakes. The *interaction model* is the product.

**4. Onboarding friction is not laziness. It's a login the parent can't get past.**
This was the biggest correction. I had assumed the missing-document problem was Arnab's father not caring. It was a password he didn't have and shame about admitting it [00:01:39]. The fix was one sentence — forward the renewal email, don't download anything — and he agreed in eight seconds. So the onboarding step "resolve missing policies through the person who holds them" must generate a *specific, tiny, forwardable ask* aimed at the holder, in the holder's language, and it must be phrased so the holder isn't embarrassed. Not "collect your documents." Generic asks fail. Specific asks land.

**5. The human-on-the-line has evidence behind it now.**
Arnab at [00:15:19]: repeating the same five facts to five different people while standing in a hospital corridor. At [00:15:31] he interrupted me to say he'd pay for someone who already had the context. That's mode four, and the thing being sold is not "a human" — every insurer offers a helpline. It's a human who does not ask you for your policy number.

**6. Willingness to pay is real in the crisis and near-zero when calm — and I have to design around that, not wish it away.**
[00:16:03]: "When there's no problem you don't remember it." So Coversaath cannot be priced as emergency insurance-for-insurance. The calm-month value has to be mode one and mode three — knowing where your family stands, and being told what not to buy — because those are the only things someone will pay for on a Tuesday when nobody is sick. The emergency button is what makes them keep paying. It is not what makes them start.

**7. Data quality is worse than I assumed and the three-state model is non-negotiable.**
Seven of nineteen collected documents have a field on the schedule that doesn't match reality — including a sister's date of birth off by six years, which is the kind of thing that becomes a dispute at exactly the wrong moment. Every field has to be *confirmed*, *uncertain*, or *missing*, with the reason attached. Admit first, optimise later. A confident wrong answer at a hospital counter is worse than no product at all.

**Still unresolved after this round:**
- I have no evidence on the buy-or-renew mode. Nobody in this round was actively shopping. Round 3 needs two or three respondents mid-purchase.
- Vikas's line about not being able to file off a third-party sheet needs testing at two more hospitals. If desks generally won't look at it, mode two's output format has to change.
- I still don't know what happens when the household disagrees — a father who doesn't want his son seeing his medical history. Permissions were designed on assumption, not evidence.

### Tab-by-tab: what is now evidenced

**Tab 1 — My coverage.** Evidenced in Rounds 2 and 3, strongly. Arnab [T1 00:01:34] couldn't confirm his own sister was on the family policy. Every single respondent overstated their coverage on the intake form and understated their exposure. The per-member view is non-negotiable and the three-state model (confirmed / uncertain / missing) survives contact with reality — 7 of 19 collected documents had a schedule field that didn't match the family's own account of it.

**Tab 2 — Plan an expense.** Evidenced. Arnab's ₹80,000 loss came from a ₹1,200 room decision made with no information [T1 00:06:15]. Two new requirements from Round 3: the output must include a *document list specific to the case type* — Sourav's claim sat for two months because nobody told him a road accident needs an FIR copy [T5 00:07:17] — and it must state the upfront cash number, because Vikas says families arrive empty-handed and then hunt for an ATM [T2 00:03:42].

**Tab 3 — Buy or renew.** Now evidenced, and this was the Round 2 gap. Meghna was three months into being sold a 20L top-up with a 10L deductible on top of an 8L base — a two-lakh dead zone she discovered live on the call [T4 00:01:34] — while both her parents sat on a policy she couldn't name the sum insured of [T4 00:03:10]. Recommendations without citations are useless in a family context. Second: **the output must be allowed to say "not this, and not now."** She would have redirected the spend to her parents [T4 00:05:37]. **Tab 4 — Emergency help.** Now evidenced properly, and the design was wrong before this. Sourav lost seven hours between 2 a.m. and 9 a.m. because his brother's phone was locked and the employer's reception wanted an employee ID he didn't have [T5 00:03:08]. ## Method, evidence status and design inferences

**Research context.** Conversations ran from 5 to 8 September 2026 and came mostly through friends,
family, relatives and their acquaintances. Treat the patterns as directional, not as a prevalence study.

### What is recorded in the transcripts

- Families cannot always name the policy, covered members, limits or documents when a hospital asks.
- Room-rent caps, exclusions, deductibles and cash deposits can create a gap even when a policy exists.
- Employer cover, retail cover, top-ups and card benefits sit in separate silos.
- During an emergency, people need one human route with the authorised context already available. They
  will not complete a multi-screen app flow.
- Permission is personal. A member may allow a human emergency operator to see full details while showing
  family only cover amount and funding route.

### Design implications

- Start by rebuilding existing cover before recommending another policy.
- Show source-linked numbers for every person: confirmed, uncertain or missing.
- Compare base cover, employer cover, top-up deductibles, exclusions, likely cash needs and renewal cost.
  Do not promise claim approval or savings.
- At a trigger such as renewal, job change, family change or planned treatment, produce the next actions,
  document list, owners and deadlines.
- Keep an emergency route to a human who can interpret the file and coordinate next steps. Admission and
  treatment come first.
- Use field-level, viewer-specific permissions with a member-approved emergency override.

### Still unresolved

- Whether households will share records and pay during calm months.
- Whether existing advisers, HR teams and hospital desks already solve enough of this.
- Whether portability, card benefits and emergency handoffs work in real cases.
- How people respond when the honest answer is that a parent is under-insured or uninsurable at an
  affordable premium.
- Prevalence beyond this supplied set. These transcripts do not establish market size.

**Overall conclusion.** Across these conversations the repeated observation is not "people need another
policy". It is that existing cover is fragmented, hard to operate under pressure and rarely compared
across household members. The inference is a testable product direction: reconstruct first, show
source-linked gaps and next actions, then offer a human route. This remains a hypothesis until real,
consented cases are observed at scale.

**Publication rule.** Before public use, verify provenance, confirm consent, redact identifying details
and retain the original evidence. Keep source facts, user-reported experience, simulations, assumptions
and proposals separate. Mrs. Ghosh asked that the contents of her session not reach her son. That has
been honoured and must remain honoured in every derived document.
